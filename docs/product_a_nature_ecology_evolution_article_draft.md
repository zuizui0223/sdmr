# Predictive success does not identify ecological necessity in species distribution models

Status: **Nature Ecology & Evolution Article draft / reporting only / Product-A endpoint unchanged**

## Abstract

Species distribution models are routinely selected for predictive performance and then interpreted ecologically, although predictive adequacy and ecological necessity are distinct inferential targets. We developed a prospectively sealed occurrence-only framework that asks whether a proposed environmental-process claim survives falsification against adequate alternatives. Controlled-truth experiments first confirmed a known limitation of discrimination-based selection: predictive transfer and stable response surfaces could coexist with incorrect process attribution. More importantly, restricting inference to models with better ecological recovery could itself create false necessary-process claims. Replacing winner-based interpretation with falsification-first process exclusion and explicit unresolved states avoided false necessity and, after prospective calibration and deterministic execution, recovered stable process cores across 60 unused cases from six niche families with precision 0.9889 and recall 0.9833. Exact fitted-model consensus occurred in 38/60 cases but process-set consensus in 50/60. A fresh empirical endpoint across 12 plant taxa, three split seeds and three accessible-area assumptions revealed a complementary limit: ecological and AUC selection collapsed to the identical candidate in all 108 matched cells, so strict empirical improvement was not supported. Ecological necessity therefore requires identification against adequate alternatives, not prediction-based winner selection.

Ecological models are often asked to do two jobs at once: predict observations and explain the environmental structure behind them. Species distribution models (SDMs) make this distinction especially consequential because they are widely used to infer environmental constraints from occurrence data even when direct experiments are infeasible. Yet predictive discrimination and ecological interpretation are not the same target. Elith and Leathwick framed explanation and prediction as distinct roles of SDMs more than a decade ago, and Warren, Matzke and Iglesias subsequently showed with virtual species that discrimination accuracy can be largely uninformative about the functional accuracy of fitted environmental responses [1,2]. Modern tools such as ENMeval have made spatial partitioning, model tuning and reproducible evaluation substantially more rigorous [3]. The unresolved problem is what follows after a model has passed those predictive and functional checks: when can an environmental process be called necessary for the ecological interpretation?

That question becomes difficult whenever environmental representations are observationally substitutable. Correlated climatic variables, derived exposure metrics, topographic surrogates and composite indices can yield similar predictions while implying different ecological interpretations. Work on Rashomon/model-class reasoning has formalized the broader statistical fact that many well-performing models can support different variable-importance claims [4,5]. In SDMs, positional uncertainty and predictor autocorrelation can further distort inferred species–environment relationships or variable importance even when predictions remain usable [6,7]. These results motivate uncertainty-aware interpretation, but they do not by themselves define ecological necessity.

We treat necessity here as an **identification claim under a declared representation system**, not as proof of a complete causal mechanism. A process is not necessary merely because it appears in the best model, has high variable importance, or occurs in every model retained after performance filtering. It is necessary only to the extent that an adequate ecological explanation becomes unavailable when the declared information associated with that process is prospectively excluded. If alternative explanations survive, necessity is refuted; if the evidence or calibration required to decide is insufficient, the state remains unresolved rather than being converted into absence.

We developed this logic through a prospectively protected sequence of occurrence-only SDM tests. The programme began with a conventional objective—choose one complete model-building procedure that transfers to sealed spatial blocks and unseen taxa—but whole spatial blocks were assigned to model-pool or sealed roles before tuning, accessible-area assumptions were treated as sensitivity conditions rather than optimized choices, unseen taxa were excluded from procedure selection, and non-promotion was defined as a valid outcome. These barriers allowed increasingly strong interpretations to be falsified without repairing earlier outcomes after inspection. We asked four questions: can predictive or functional recovery identify generating process information; can agreement among ecologically good models establish necessity; can falsification-first exclusion recover process information without false necessity; and does the resulting selection logic create an independent empirical contrast in fresh plant occurrence data?

## Results

### Prediction and stable surfaces did not identify process truth

The first controlled-truth tests separated predictive recovery from ecological attribution. Changing the selection objective from predictive discrimination to ecological recovery did not reliably create a distinct winning procedure, and a subsequent stability test exposed the deeper problem: a candidate could recover withheld occurrence environments or produce a stable response surface while attributing the pattern to the wrong generating environmental processes. This reproduces and extends the distinction between discrimination and functional accuracy established previously [2]: even when the fitted environmental surface itself is stable, the process interpretation can remain wrong.

Prediction transfer, response-surface stability and process identification were therefore separable targets even behind protected information barriers. Prediction remained a necessary adequacy layer, but the ecological estimand shifted from the identity of one winning model to the process information defensible across adequate alternative explanations.

### Model-set sharpening could create false necessity

We next replaced a single winner with a set of adequate ecological models and asked whether better ecological recovery could safely sharpen that set. Models were filtered to an ecological Pareto front and shared processes among the retained models were initially interpreted as necessary, with between-model spread used to describe response uncertainty.

The procedure became sharper in every controlled-truth panel, but it also became wrong. Pareto pruning lost generating-process or response-boundary coverage and could create a false necessary-process core. Thus the problem was not simply that predictive criteria fail to recover ecological responses. **Even selection on ecological-recovery criteria can manufacture false necessity when the retained model class excludes viable alternatives.** Agreement among retained fitted models was conditional on the retention rule, and the range among a selected subset of models was not a complete uncertainty interval.

This result distinguishes the present problem from conventional variable-importance or Rashomon summaries [4,5]. Those approaches ask how importance varies across a class of good models. Here the target is stronger: whether a process-level necessity claim survives when the information carrying that process is made unavailable under a frozen design.

### Falsification-first identification became safe and sharp

We therefore replaced agreement-based necessity with falsification-first exclusion. Boundary uncertainty was calibrated separately from discovery evidence. If the predeclared calibration support for a required response key did not exist, the result was unavailable rather than negative.

The first process-exclusion test already exposed an informative asymmetry. All nine validation taxa had complete process certificates, false-required processes were zero and possible-process recall was 1.0, but the full process-plus-boundary product remained unavailable because three required soil response keys lacked discovery-calibrated intervals. A subsequent calibration stage retained the preregistered support threshold and left fresh validation unopened when that threshold was not met. These states were neither positive nor negative ecological outcomes; they recorded that the requested claim was not identified by the available evidence.

After calibration redundancy was supplied prospectively, the next controlled-truth validation produced complete process and boundary certificates, no false-required processes and possible-process recall of 1.0. The cost was broad uncertainty: possible-process precision was approximately 0.467 and calibrated response intervals were wider than those from the complete-adequate model set. Refusing to remove plausible alternatives protected truth but initially sacrificed sharpness.

A deterministic successor showed that this trade-off was not intrinsic. Sixty unused controlled-truth cases represented six niche-generating families and were evaluated in two independent processes. All compared floating and discrete outputs were exactly identical, including 7,140 candidate-fold rows and 173,880 floating values; the maximum absolute and relative difference was 0.0.

Stable process cores were both accurate and robust across generating structures. Mean precision was 0.9889 and recall and F1 were 0.9833. Four niche families—asymmetric, Gaussian, interaction and observation-confounded—had mean precision and recall of 1.0. The omitted-driver family retained precision 1.0 but recall declined to 0.90, whereas the soft-threshold family retained recall 1.0 but precision declined to 0.933. Missing driver information therefore primarily left truth unresolved, while gradual threshold structure allowed an extra process to persist.

Process identity was also more stable than exact model identity. Exact fitted-model consensus occurred in 38 of 60 cases, whereas process-set consensus occurred in 50 of 60. In asymmetric niches, model consensus was 0.40 but process-set consensus 0.80; in Gaussian niches the corresponding values were 0.50 and 0.90. Observation-process correction activated in all 10 observation-confounded cases and in none of the other 50. Process information could therefore remain identifiable even when a unique fitted model was not.

### Fresh occurrence data revealed observational equivalence

Controlled truth can evaluate generating-process recovery, whereas empirical occurrence data cannot provide a literal process answer key. We therefore used a final fresh plant-data endpoint for a narrower test: would the ecological selection rule instantiate a different, independently better empirical solution than an AUC-selected comparator behind the same prospective barriers?

The empirical lane separated structural and technical failure states from ecological outcomes. Spatial validation geometry was checked before environmental values were opened, and runtime failures before sealed ecological evidence were classified as technical rather than ecological results. A runtime-only successor eventually completed the full preregistered endpoint: 12 taxa in each of three split seeds and three 150, 300 and 500 km accessible-area conditions.

The formal superiority result was negative. Prediction adequacy passed, the ecological role was nondominated relative to AUC in all three parts, and process-status reproducibility passed, but strict ecological improvement occurred in none of the three parts. The endpoint was therefore `empirical_confirmation_not_supported`, followed by a separate `not_promoted` decision.

The frozen full-denominator artifacts reveal why the empirical comparison contained no realized contrast. Across all 108 matched taxon × accessible-area × seed cells, the ecological and AUC roles selected the same candidate in 108/108 cases and the same predictor set in 108/108 cases. Every cell selected `all|logit_l2_C0.1_degree1_rs0`, and all audited sealed metrics were consequently identical between roles.

This does not show that AUC identifies ecological truth. It shows that, for this occurrence corpus and candidate system, two distinct selection objectives were **observationally equivalent at the level of the selected model**. The preregistered superiority claim was tested and not supported; the stronger empirical question—whether falsification-first identification outperforms prediction-based selection when the two rules genuinely disagree—was not identified because no selector disagreement occurred.

## Discussion

The prospective evidence sequence establishes a distinction that is narrower than causal explanation but stronger than conventional model selection. Prediction can be validated against withheld observations. Functional response recovery can be evaluated under known truth. Ecological necessity, however, cannot be read from either alone. Nor can it be manufactured by retaining only high-performing models, because performance-based sharpening can remove viable alternatives and create false certainty. Under observational substitutability, necessity is an identification problem: does an adequate explanation remain after the declared information associated with a proposed process is removed?

This framing builds directly on prior demonstrations that discrimination accuracy can fail to identify useful environmental response functions [1,2] and on model-class approaches that preserve uncertainty across many good predictors [4,5]. Its additional step is a falsification criterion for **process-information necessity**. The target is not whether a variable is important to one or many predictive models, but whether a process-level ecological claim remains indispensable once adequate alternatives are allowed to survive. The controlled-truth failure of Pareto sharpening is essential evidence for this distinction: better ecological recovery did not protect against false necessity.

Set-valued outcomes are therefore substantive ecological results rather than incomplete model selection. `necessary`, `possible/substitutable`, `contested` and `unresolved` encode different identification states. The controlled-truth results show that this caution need not make inference uninformative. Once calibration and computational identity were prospectively controlled, stable process cores were recovered with approximately 0.99 precision and 0.98 recall across six generating structures, and process identity was more stable than exact fitted-model identity.

The empirical collapse adds a complementary limit. Observational data may fail not only to reveal literal process truth, but even to generate the model contrast needed to compare alternative selection principles. In such cases an empirical tie is not evidence that ecological necessity has been identified. Here, 108/108 matched cells instantiated exactly the same model under ecological and AUC selection. That result bounds, rather than rescues, the known-truth claim.

Two boundaries remain. First, the fresh plant data contain no literal generating-process truth, so controlled-truth identification performance cannot be presented as direct empirical process validation. Second, the current process registry does not implement a complete closure over every real-world proxy or composite representation. A future prospective test of process-information indispensability would need to declare that representation closure before any new outcome is opened. These limits prevent a causal reading of the present certificates.

Product A therefore changes the inferential object rather than promoting a universally superior tuner. For observational ecological models with correlated or substitutable representations, the central question is not which model wins, but which ecological claims remain defensible after adequate alternatives have been allowed to survive.
