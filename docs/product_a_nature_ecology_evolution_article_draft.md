# Predictive success does not identify ecological necessity in species distribution models

Status: **Nature Ecology & Evolution Article draft / reporting only / Product-A endpoint unchanged**

## Abstract

Ecologists often infer environmental drivers from models selected for predictive performance, although prediction and ecological necessity are different inferential targets. We developed a prospectively sealed occurrence-only framework that tests necessity by asking whether adequate ecological explanations survive when declared process information is excluded. Known-truth experiments showed that predictive transfer and stable response surfaces could coexist with incorrect process attribution, while pruning to ecologically better models could create false necessary-process claims. Falsification-first exclusion with explicit unresolved states avoided false necessity and, after prospective calibration and deterministic execution, recovered stable process cores across 60 unused cases from six niche families with precision 0.9889 and recall 0.9833. Exact model consensus occurred in 38/60 cases but process-set consensus in 50/60, showing that ecological information can remain identifiable without a unique fitted model. A fresh empirical endpoint across 12 plant taxa, three split seeds and three accessible-area assumptions yielded a different limit: ecological and AUC selection chose the identical candidate in all 108 matched cells, so strict empirical improvement was not supported. Ecological model interpretation therefore requires identification against adequate alternatives, not prediction-based winner selection.

## Main text

Ecological models are often asked to do two jobs at once. They must predict where organisms occur, and they are then interpreted to identify the environmental conditions that structure those occurrences. The first task can be assessed directly against withheld observations. The second is harder: correlated climate variables, derived exposures, topographic surrogates and composite indices can produce similar predictions while implying different biological explanations. A model can therefore predict well without uniquely identifying which environmental information is necessary for the ecological pattern.

Species distribution models (SDMs) make this distinction especially consequential because occurrence-only data combine an ecological process with an observation process. AUC, Boyce-type statistics, omission rates, information criteria and spatial cross-validation provide useful information about fitted models, but none is itself a biological answer key. Sampling effort, detectability and accessible-area definition can alter record discrimination without changing the underlying environmental response, while multiple environmental representations can remain observationally substitutable.

We asked whether niche interpretation could instead be treated as an identification problem. The project began prospectively with a conventional model-selection objective: choose one complete model-building procedure that transfers to sealed spatial blocks and unseen taxa. From the outset, however, whole spatial blocks were assigned to model-pool or sealed roles before tuning, accessible-area assumptions were treated as sensitivity conditions rather than optimized choices, unseen taxa were excluded from procedure selection, and failure to meet promotion thresholds was defined as a valid result. These information barriers allowed subsequent known-truth tests to falsify increasingly strong interpretations without repairing earlier outcomes after inspection.

The resulting framework asks a different question from conventional winner selection. A process is not considered necessary because it appears in the best model, or even because it appears in every retained good model. Necessity is challenged by removing the declared information associated with that process and asking whether an adequate ecological explanation remains. If alternatives survive, necessity is refuted; if evidence or calibration is insufficient, the state remains unresolved rather than being converted to absence. We evaluated this logic through a prospective sequence of controlled-truth and fresh empirical tests.

## Prediction and stable surfaces did not identify process truth

The first controlled-truth tests separated predictive recovery from ecological attribution. Changing the selection objective from predictive discrimination to ecological recovery did not reliably create a distinct winning procedure, and a subsequent stability test exposed the deeper problem: a candidate could recover withheld occurrence environments or produce a stable response surface while attributing the pattern to the wrong generating environmental processes. Prediction transfer, response-surface stability and process identification were therefore empirically separable even when all were evaluated on protected evidence.

This result invalidated the assumption that an ecologically interpreted SDM can be justified by finding a better single winner. Prediction remained a necessary adequacy layer, but the ecological target became the information that could be defended across adequate alternative explanations.

## Model-set sharpening could create false necessity

We next replaced a single winner with a set of adequate ecological models and asked whether better ecological recovery could safely sharpen that set. Models were filtered to a Pareto front and shared processes among the retained models were initially interpreted as necessary, with between-model spread used to describe response uncertainty.

The procedure became sharper in every controlled-truth panel, but it also became wrong. Pareto pruning lost generating-process or response-boundary coverage and could create a false necessary-process core. Agreement among retained fitted models was therefore conditional on the retention rule rather than evidence of biological necessity. Likewise, the range among a selected subset of models was not a complete uncertainty interval. A narrower model class could be simultaneously more precise and more anti-conservative.

This failure motivated a falsification-first definition of ecological necessity. Instead of asking which processes were shared by good models, we asked whether a valid ecological certificate still existed when the declared information from a process was excluded. Boundary uncertainty was calibrated separately from discovery evidence. If the necessary calibration support did not exist, the result was unavailable rather than negative.

## Falsification-first identification became safe and sharp

The first process-exclusion test already recovered an important asymmetry. All nine validation taxa had complete process certificates, false-required processes were zero and possible-process recall was 1.0, but the full process-plus-boundary product was unavailable because three required soil response keys lacked discovery-calibrated intervals. A subsequent calibration stage retained the preregistered support threshold and left fresh validation unopened when that threshold was not met. These were not model failures: they were explicit states in which the available evidence could not support the requested claim.

After calibration redundancy was supplied prospectively, the next controlled-truth validation produced complete process and boundary certificates, no false-required processes and possible-process recall of 1.0. The cost was broad uncertainty: possible-process precision was approximately 0.467 and calibrated response intervals were wider than those from the complete-adequate model set. This was a deliberate consequence of refusing to remove plausible explanations merely to obtain a narrower result.

A later deterministic successor showed that this safety–sharpness trade-off was not intrinsic. Sixty unused controlled-truth cases represented six niche-generating families and were evaluated in two independent processes. All compared floating and discrete scientific outputs were exactly identical between processes, including 7,140 candidate-fold rows and 173,880 floating values, with maximum absolute and relative difference 0.0.

Stable process cores were both accurate and broadly robust across generating structures. Mean precision was 0.9889 and recall and F1 were 0.9833. Four niche families—asymmetric, Gaussian, interaction and observation-confounded—had mean precision and recall of 1.0. The omitted-driver family retained precision 1.0 but recall declined to 0.90, whereas the soft-threshold family retained recall 1.0 but precision declined to 0.933. These boundary cases identify different failure modes: missing driver information primarily leaves truth unresolved, whereas gradual threshold structure can allow an extra process to persist.

The distinction between model identity and ecological information was also visible directly. Exact fitted-model consensus occurred in 38 of 60 cases, but process-set consensus occurred in 50 of 60. In asymmetric niches, model consensus was 0.40 while process-set consensus was 0.80; in Gaussian niches the corresponding values were 0.50 and 0.90. Ecological process information could therefore remain stable even when the exact fitted model did not. Observation-process correction was similarly specific: it activated in all 10 observation-confounded cases and in none of the other 50 cases.

## Fresh occurrence data revealed observational equivalence

Controlled truth can establish whether generating processes are recovered, but empirical occurrence data do not reveal literal process truth. We therefore used a final fresh plant-data endpoint to ask a narrower question: does the ecological selection rule produce independent improvement over an AUC-selected comparator when both are evaluated behind the same prospective information barriers?

The empirical lane preserved structural and technical failure states separately from scientific outcomes. Spatial validation geometry was checked before environmental values were opened, and earlier runtime failures before sealed ecological evidence were classified as technical rather than ecological results. A runtime-only successor eventually completed the full preregistered endpoint: 12 taxa in each of three split seeds and three 150, 300 and 500 km accessible-area conditions.

The formal result did not support empirical superiority. Prediction adequacy passed, the ecological role was nondominated relative to AUC in all three parts, and process-status reproducibility passed, but strict ecological improvement occurred in none of the three parts. The preregistered endpoint was therefore `empirical_confirmation_not_supported`, followed by a separate `not_promoted` decision.

Inspection of the frozen full-denominator artifacts explains this non-support more sharply. Across all 108 matched taxon × accessible-area × seed cells, the ecological and AUC roles selected the same candidate in 108/108 cases and the same selected-predictor set in 108/108 cases. Every cell used the candidate `all|logit_l2_C0.1_degree1_rs0`. The two roles were consequently identical for the audited sealed prediction and ecological metrics.

Thus the empirical endpoint did not show that AUC recovers ecological truth better, nor did it provide a realized comparison between two genuinely different fitted models. It showed that, for this frozen occurrence corpus, two distinct selection objectives collapsed to the same empirical solution. The formal superiority claim was tested and not supported, while the stronger question—whether falsification-first process identification outperforms prediction-based selection when the two rules genuinely disagree in real ecological data—remains unidentified by this endpoint.

## Discussion

The development sequence identifies a general inferential problem in ecological modelling. Prediction can be validated against withheld observations, but ecological necessity cannot be read from prediction alone. Nor can necessity be manufactured by restricting attention to a subset of high-performing models, because agreement within a selected model class can exclude viable alternatives and create false certainty. Ecological necessity instead requires a falsification target: whether an adequate explanation survives when the declared information associated with a proposed process is unavailable.

This distinction matters beyond a particular SDM implementation. Many ecological analyses combine correlated measurements, proxies and composite representations and then interpret selected variables as mechanistic evidence. Whenever multiple representations are observationally substitutable, a winning model answers a comparative performance question but need not answer an identification question. Set-valued outcomes—necessary, possible or substitutable, contested and unresolved—are therefore not a failure of model selection; they are the natural output of incomplete identification.

The controlled-truth results show that this caution need not make inference uninformative. Once calibration and computational identity were prospectively controlled, stable process cores were recovered with approximately 0.99 precision and 0.98 recall across multiple niche-generating structures. Process identity was also substantially more stable than exact model identity. The empirical result establishes the complementary limitation: observational data may not even create the selector contrast required to compare alternative tuning principles. In such cases a null difference between selectors is not evidence that ecological necessity has been established; it is evidence that the available observational information does not distinguish the competing procedures.

Two boundaries remain important. First, the empirical plant data do not provide literal generating-process truth, so controlled-truth identification performance cannot be claimed as direct empirical process validation. Second, the current process registry does not implement a complete real-world closure over every possible proxy or composite representation. A future prospective test of process-information indispensability would need to declare that representation closure before any new outcome is opened.

Product A therefore resolves a methodological rather than a universal biological claim. It makes niche-driver identification measurable under controlled truth and shows prospectively why predictive success, model agreement and ecological necessity must be separated. The final empirical non-promotion is part of that result rather than an exception to it. For observational ecological models, the central question is not which model wins, but which ecological claims remain defensible after adequate alternatives have been allowed to survive.
