# Predictive success does not identify ecological necessity in species distribution models

Status: **Nature Ecology & Evolution Article draft / reporting only / Product-A endpoint unchanged**

## Abstract

Species distribution models are routinely selected for predictive performance and then interpreted ecologically, although predictive adequacy and ecological process identification are distinct targets. We developed a prospectively sealed occurrence-only framework to separate these claims. Controlled-truth experiments showed that predictive transfer and stable response surfaces could coexist with incorrect process attribution, and that filtering to models with better ecological recovery could create false necessary-process claims. Process exclusion then eliminated false-required claims but remained deliberately broad. A separate consensus-first certificate recovered the complete hidden generating-process set in 55/60 unused cases. Even when canonical and robust ecological selectors chose different fitted models, the stable process set still exactly matched truth in 19/22 cases. Soil was the only process whose truth status varied: among 10 true-soil cases, 7 were stable and 3 contested; among 50 false-soil cases, 2 were incorrectly stable, 7 contested and 41 absent from both selectors. A fresh empirical endpoint across 12 plant taxa produced identical ecological and AUC-selected candidates in all 108 matched cells, so strict empirical improvement was not supported. Ecological identification can therefore recover process information beyond exact model identity while preserving explicit uncertainty and empirical non-identification.

Ecological models are often asked to do two jobs at once: predict observations and explain the environmental structure behind them. Species distribution models (SDMs) make this distinction especially consequential because they are widely used to infer environmental constraints from occurrence data even when direct experiments are infeasible. Yet predictive discrimination and ecological interpretation are not the same target. Elith and Leathwick framed explanation and prediction as distinct roles of SDMs more than a decade ago, and Warren, Matzke and Iglesias subsequently showed with virtual species that discrimination accuracy can be largely uninformative about the functional accuracy of fitted environmental responses [1,2]. Modern tools such as ENMeval have made spatial partitioning, model tuning and reproducible evaluation substantially more rigorous [3]. The unresolved problem is what follows after a model has passed those predictive and functional checks: when can an environmental process be called necessary for the ecological interpretation?

That question becomes difficult whenever environmental representations are observationally substitutable. Correlated climatic variables, derived exposure metrics, topographic surrogates and composite indices can yield similar predictions while implying different ecological interpretations. Work on Rashomon/model-class reasoning has formalized the broader statistical fact that many well-performing models can support different variable-importance claims [4,5]. In SDMs, positional uncertainty and predictor autocorrelation can further distort inferred species–environment relationships or variable importance even when predictions remain usable [6,7]. These results motivate uncertainty-aware interpretation, but they do not by themselves define ecological necessity.

We treat ecological identification as containing at least two distinct process-level claims. **Necessity** is an identification claim under a declared representation system: a process is necessary only to the extent that an adequate ecological explanation becomes unavailable when the declared information associated with that process is prospectively excluded. **Process stability** is a different claim: whether independently defined ecological selectors support the same process information even when they do not select the same fitted model. These estimands are complementary but not interchangeable. Neither is proof of a complete causal mechanism or fundamental niche.

We developed this logic through a prospectively protected sequence of occurrence-only SDM tests. The programme began with a conventional objective—choose one complete model-building procedure that transfers to sealed spatial blocks and unseen taxa—but whole spatial blocks were assigned to model-pool or sealed roles before tuning, accessible-area assumptions were treated as sensitivity conditions rather than optimized choices, unseen taxa were excluded from procedure selection, and non-promotion was defined as a valid outcome. These barriers allowed increasingly strong interpretations to be falsified without repairing earlier outcomes after inspection. We asked five questions: can predictive or functional recovery identify generating process information; can agreement among ecologically good models establish necessity; can falsification-first exclusion control false necessity; can process information remain stable without exact model identity; and does the resulting ecological selection logic create an independent empirical contrast in fresh plant occurrence data?

## Results

### Prediction and stable surfaces did not identify process truth

The first controlled-truth tests separated predictive recovery from ecological attribution. Changing the selection objective from predictive discrimination to ecological recovery did not reliably create a distinct winning procedure, and a subsequent stability test exposed the deeper problem: a candidate could recover withheld occurrence environments or produce a stable response surface while attributing the pattern to the wrong generating environmental processes. This reproduces and extends the distinction between discrimination and functional accuracy established previously [2]: even when the fitted environmental surface itself is stable, the process interpretation can remain wrong.

Prediction transfer, response-surface stability and process identification were therefore separable targets even behind protected information barriers. Prediction remained a necessary adequacy layer, but the ecological estimand shifted from the identity of one winning model to the process information defensible across adequate alternative explanations.

### Model-set sharpening could create false necessity

We next replaced a single winner with a set of adequate ecological models and asked whether better ecological recovery could safely sharpen that set. Models were filtered to an ecological Pareto front and shared processes among the retained models were initially interpreted as necessary, with between-model spread used to describe response uncertainty.

The procedure became sharper in every controlled-truth panel, but it also became wrong. Pareto pruning lost generating-process or response-boundary coverage and could create a false necessary-process core. Thus the problem was not simply that predictive criteria fail to recover ecological responses. **Even selection on ecological-recovery criteria can manufacture false necessity when the retained model class excludes viable alternatives.** Agreement among retained fitted models was conditional on the retention rule, and the range among a selected subset of models was not a complete uncertainty interval.

This result distinguishes the present problem from conventional variable-importance or Rashomon summaries [4,5]. Those approaches ask how importance varies across a class of good models. Here the target is stronger: whether a process-level necessity claim survives when the information carrying that process is made unavailable under a frozen design.

### Falsification-first exclusion controlled false necessity but remained broad

We therefore replaced agreement-based necessity with falsification-first exclusion. Process status distinguished claims that were refuted as necessary, required by the frozen evidence contract or unresolved. Boundary uncertainty was calibrated separately from discovery evidence. If the predeclared calibration support for a required response key did not exist, the result was unavailable rather than negative.

The first process-exclusion test already exposed an informative asymmetry. All nine validation taxa had complete process certificates, false-required processes were zero and possible-process recall was 1.0, but the full process-plus-boundary product remained unavailable because three required soil response keys lacked discovery-calibrated intervals. A subsequent calibration stage retained the preregistered support threshold and left fresh validation unopened when that threshold was not met. These states were neither positive nor negative ecological outcomes; they recorded that the requested claim was not identified by the available evidence.

After calibration redundancy was supplied prospectively, the next controlled-truth validation produced complete process and boundary certificates, no false-required processes and possible-process recall of 1.0. The cost was broad uncertainty: possible-process precision was approximately 0.467 and calibrated response intervals were wider than those from the complete-adequate model set. In all nine validation taxa the `required_processes` set remained empty. Thus this branch solved a safety problem—preventing unsupported necessity claims—rather than positively isolating a required ecological driver.

This branch therefore supports a specific conclusion about **necessity**: process-information exclusion can control false-required claims when calibration is adequate, but a safe identified set may remain broad and can contain no positively required process. The positive process-recovery result comes from the separate consensus-first certificate below.

### Process information remained stable without unique model identity

We next asked a complementary and more constructive question: even when necessity remains set-valued, can the generating process set itself be recovered more reliably than exact fitted-model identity? The ecological certificate compared two independently defined ecological selectors—a canonical niche-recovery selector and a perturbation-robust niche-recovery selector. Its `stable_process_core` was the intersection of the process sets supported by those two selectors; it was not a process-exclusion necessity set.

Before evaluating that certificate on new truth, an implementation-level audit exposed a scientific reproducibility vulnerability. Independent process reconstruction changed one discrete selected-predictor output because the frozen liblinear fit did not define an explicit random-state identity. The successor therefore prospectively fixed estimator and selection-process random states before unused known-truth evidence was opened.

Sixty unused controlled-truth cases represented six niche-generating families and were evaluated in two independent computational processes. All compared floating and discrete outputs were exactly identical, including 7,140 candidate-fold rows and 173,880 floating values; the maximum absolute and relative difference was 0.0.

The stable process core **exactly equalled the complete hidden generating-process set in 55/60 cases (91.7%)**. By comparison, the canonical selector process set was exact in 52/60 and the perturbation-robust selector process set in 54/60. Exact fitted candidates differed between the two ecological selectors in 22/60 cases; even within those model-disagreement cases, the stable process core still exactly matched the complete generating-process set in **19/22 (86.4%)**. All 40 cases from the asymmetric, Gaussian, interaction and observation-confounded families had exact stable-core process recovery. The five errors were confined to the omitted-driver family (three missed soil processes) and the soft-threshold family (two falsely retained soil processes).

The process-level content of this result was also explicit. Temperature and water were true generating processes in all 60 cases and were retained in the stable process core in all 60; these invariant processes therefore demonstrate retention rather than presence-versus-absence discrimination. **Soil was the process whose truth status varied and thus supplied the selective identification test.** Soil was truly generating in 10 cases: it was stable in 7, contested in 3 and absent from both ecological selector process sets in 0. Soil was non-generating in 50 cases: it was incorrectly stable in 2, contested in 7 and absent from both selectors in 41. Thus every true-soil case was retained either as stable or contested, while only two false-soil cases were promoted to the stable core.

A worked case shows what the certificate actually identifies. In the asymmetric family at seed 3103, the canonical selector chose `climate_soil_quadratic` and supported `{soil, temperature, water}`, whereas the robust selector chose `tw_quadratic` and supported `{temperature, water}`. The certificate returned `{temperature, water}` as stable and `{soil}` as contested, exactly matching the hidden generating set despite disagreement between the fitted models. The converse failure envelopes were equally explicit: in an omitted-driver case true soil fell to contested rather than stable, whereas in two soft-threshold cases false soil survived both selectors and entered the stable core.

Pooled stable-core precision was 0.9889 and recall and F1 were 0.9833, but these averages are secondary to the exact-set and process-specific results above because temperature and water did not vary in truth status across the frozen generator suite. Exact fitted-model consensus occurred in 38/60 cases, whereas process-set consensus occurred in 50/60. Observation-process correction activated in all 10 observation-confounded cases and in none of the other 50. The concrete result is therefore bounded but positive: complete generating-process sets can be recovered in most unused known-truth cases even when the exact fitted models disagree, with the remaining errors traceable to a specific varying process and generator class.

### Fresh occurrence data revealed observational equivalence

Controlled truth can evaluate generating-process recovery, whereas empirical occurrence data cannot provide a literal process answer key. We therefore used a final fresh plant-data endpoint for a narrower test: would the ecological selection rule instantiate a different, independently better empirical solution than an AUC-selected comparator behind the same prospective barriers?

The empirical lane separated structural and technical failure states from ecological outcomes. Spatial validation geometry was checked before environmental values were opened, and runtime failures before sealed ecological evidence were classified as technical rather than ecological results. A runtime-only successor eventually completed the full preregistered endpoint: 12 taxa in each of three split seeds and three 150, 300 and 500 km accessible-area conditions.

The formal superiority result was negative. Prediction adequacy passed, the ecological role was nondominated relative to AUC in all three parts, and process-status reproducibility passed, but strict ecological improvement occurred in none of the three parts. The endpoint was therefore `empirical_confirmation_not_supported`, followed by a separate `not_promoted` decision.

The frozen full-denominator artifacts reveal why the empirical comparison contained no realized contrast. Across all 108 matched taxon × accessible-area × seed cells, the ecological and AUC roles selected the same candidate in 108/108 cases and the same predictor set in 108/108 cases. Every cell selected `all|logit_l2_C0.1_degree1_rs0`, and all audited sealed metrics were consequently identical between roles.

This does not show that AUC identifies ecological truth. It shows that, for this occurrence corpus and candidate system, two distinct selection objectives were **observationally equivalent at the level of the selected model**. The preregistered superiority claim was tested and not supported; the stronger empirical question—whether ecological identification yields a different empirical solution when selection rules genuinely disagree—was not identified because no selector disagreement occurred.

## Discussion

The prospective evidence sequence establishes a distinction that is narrower than causal explanation but stronger than conventional model selection. Prediction can be validated against withheld observations. Functional response recovery can be evaluated under known truth. Ecological identification then separates at least two process-level questions that conventional winner selection tends to collapse: **is a process required under a declared evidence system, and can the generating process set be recovered robustly across defensible ecological selectors?**

The necessity branch is falsification-first. A process cannot be declared necessary merely because it appears in a winning model or across a performance-filtered model set. The controlled-truth Pareto result showed why: performance-based sharpening can remove viable alternatives and create false certainty. Explicit process-information exclusion instead asks whether an adequate explanation survives when the declared information associated with a proposed process is removed. Under prospective calibration, this eliminated false-required claims and retained complete true-process recall, but the resulting identified sets remained broad and no process was positively required in the nine v2.6 validation taxa. The appropriate interpretation is **false-necessity control**, not positive ecological discovery.

The stability branch supplies that positive controlled-truth result. The stable process core exactly recovered the full generating-process set in 55/60 unused cases and in 19/22 cases where the two ecological selectors selected different fitted models. Its added value is therefore concrete: it can strip selector-specific process claims away from a model comparison and retain the process set shared across defensible ecological analyses. The asymmetric seed-3103 example shows the intended output directly—temperature and water stable, soil contested, truth temperature and water—even though the fitted candidates themselves differ.

The result is also narrower than the pooled precision/recall headline might suggest. Temperature and water were true in every frozen case; only soil changed truth status. Selective process identification was therefore tested most directly for soil, where seven true cases were stable, three were contested and none were completely dropped, while two of 50 non-generating cases were falsely stable. The current known-truth suite is thus a strong proof of concept for recovering process sets under model ambiguity, not a demonstration that many independently varying ecological drivers can already be identified with equal accuracy.

Set-valued outcomes are substantive ecological results rather than incomplete model selection. `required`, `refuted as necessary`, `possible/substitutable`, `contested` and `unresolved` encode different evidential roles, and no single scalar or winner should silently collapse them. The controlled-truth results show both why conservative uncertainty is needed and why ecological information need not become uninformative when exact models disagree.

The empirical collapse adds a complementary limit. Observational data may fail not only to reveal literal process truth, but even to generate the model contrast needed to compare alternative selection principles. In such cases an empirical tie is not evidence that ecological necessity has been identified. Here, 108/108 matched cells instantiated exactly the same model under ecological and AUC selection. That result bounds, rather than rescues, the controlled-truth claims.

Two boundaries remain. First, the fresh plant data contain no literal generating-process truth, so controlled-truth identification performance cannot be presented as direct empirical process validation. Second, the current process registry does not implement a complete closure over every real-world proxy or composite representation. A future prospective test of process-information indispensability would need to declare that representation closure before any new outcome is opened. These limits prevent a causal reading of the present certificates.

Product A therefore changes the inferential object rather than promoting a universally superior tuner. Its positive result is not merely that model selection can be misleading: under unused controlled truth, a set-valued process certificate recovered the complete generating-process set in most cases, including most cases in which the fitted models themselves disagreed. Its remaining challenge is empirical rather than definitional—showing which real ecological process truths can be recovered when nature supplies an independent answer key.
